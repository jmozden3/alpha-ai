## ⚡ The Alpha — Tip of the Week

**Stress-test your next big idea in 10 minutes with Perplexity**

Before you walk into a meeting, pitch a client, or commit to a direction, try this: take your idea or business case and run it through Perplexity (free at perplexity.ai) as an adversarial reviewer.

Here's how:

1. Go to perplexity.ai and start a new conversation
2. Describe your idea in 2–3 sentences — what you're proposing and what outcome you expect
3. Then ask it: *"What are the strongest arguments against this? What assumptions am I making that could be wrong? What would a skeptical stakeholder push back on?"*
4. Read the response not as an attack, but as prep work — these are the objections you'll face in the room

The difference between Perplexity and a standard ChatGPT response here is that Perplexity pulls in current information when it searches, so it can flag if there's recent market context or a counterexample that undercuts your argument. You're not just getting a generic devil's advocate — you're getting one that's looked things up.

Takes less time than a second cup of coffee. Do it before any pitch, proposal, or big decision this week.

## 🛠 Tool of the Week

**ChatGPT's Lockdown Mode — for anyone who handles sensitive information**

OpenAI quietly rolled out a feature called Lockdown Mode this week, and if your work involves confidential documents, client data, or anything you wouldn't want leaked, it's worth turning on.

**What it does:** It limits ChatGPT's ability to make outbound network requests — which closes off one of the main ways a malicious actor could theoretically trick ChatGPT into leaking your data to them via a technique called prompt injection (when hidden instructions embedded in a document or webpage try to hijack what the AI does with your information).

**How to turn it on:**
1. Open ChatGPT and go to your Settings
2. Look for the Security section
3. Enable Lockdown Mode — it's rolling out now to Free, Plus, Pro, and Business accounts

**Who should use this:** If you regularly upload contracts, client files, financial documents, or internal strategy docs to ChatGPT, this is worth enabling. OpenAI's own security team notes it's designed for people with "an elevated risk profile" — lawyers, consultants, HR professionals, anyone handling sensitive third-party information. There's a small tradeoff in functionality (some connected features may behave differently), but for sensitive work sessions, the protection is worth it.

## 💡 Prompt of the Week

**The "Book Pitch" method for thinking through any big project**

This one comes from Ethan Mollick's newsletter, which suggested pitching a project idea *to* an AI the way an author pitches a book — meaning you have to explain the concept, the audience, the core argument, and why it matters. The act of articulating all of that in one structured prompt forces clarity you don't get from just asking "help me plan X."

Use this when you're kicking off something new — a report, a proposal, a presentation, a product launch plan — and you want the AI to deeply understand the context before it helps you.

```
I want to pitch you a project the way an author pitches a book to an editor. 

Here's my concept: [describe your project in 2-3 sentences]
Here's who it's for: [describe your audience or stakeholders]
Here's the core argument or outcome: [what do you want people to think, feel, or do differently?]
Here's why it matters right now: [what's the context or urgency?]

Based on this pitch, tell me: What's compelling about it? What's missing or unclear? What questions would a skeptical stakeholder ask? Then help me build an outline or action plan.
```

The magic is in the structure. By writing out audience, argument, and urgency before asking for help, you get back something actually tailored to your situation — not a generic template.

## 🔓 Workflow Unlock

**Build a "second brain" that actually gives information back when you need it**

The graveyard of beautiful note-taking systems is real — community discussion on r/AI_Agents this week surfaced a question that a lot of people are quietly wrestling with: everyone builds an elaborate knowledge system, fills it with highlights and summaries, and then never consults it again.

Here's a simpler approach that focuses on retrieval, not storage:

**The setup:**
1. Pick one place to capture everything — could be a notes app, a simple document, even a running email thread to yourself. The tool matters less than the habit.
2. When you save something, write one sentence in your own words: *"This is useful when ___."* That's it. That's the retrieval tag.
3. Once a week (Friday afternoon works well), open your AI of choice — ChatGPT, Claude, whatever — paste in your week's notes and ask: *"Based on these notes, what's one connection or insight I might be missing? What from this week should I revisit before next week?"*

This flips the model. Instead of building an elaborate system and hoping ideas resurface, you're doing a short weekly review with AI as a thinking partner that can spot connections across your scattered notes.

The insight from that community thread: the problem isn't capturing ideas — it's making them come back at the right moment. A weekly AI-assisted review is the lowest-friction way to close that loop without building a system that takes more energy to maintain than it saves.

## 📡 Signal vs Noise

**SIGNAL: Apple is rebuilding Siri from scratch — and this time it might actually matter**

Apple announced a fundamentally new version of Siri at WWDC this week, and for the first time in years, the underlying approach looks credible. The new Siri uses a Gemini-derived model running in Apple's private cloud infrastructure, and — crucially — it uses vision AI to read what's on your screen rather than requiring every app to be individually updated to work with it. That's a practical workaround that means the new capabilities could work across your existing apps much sooner.

Why does this matter to you? If it works as described, Siri stops being a glorified timer-setter and becomes something closer to an assistant that actually understands the context of what you're doing — the email you're composing, the document you're reading, the meeting invite you just received. On-device AI that understands your screen and your data is a genuinely different kind of useful.

Worth noting: Simon Willison, who covers AI closely and was burned by Apple's 2024 announcements, is explicitly in "I'll believe it when I see it" mode on this — and that's a reasonable posture. The features are rolling out to a waitlist. Watch the early reports before rearranging how you work around it.

**NOISE: The "AI is writing most of our code" headlines**

This week brought attention to a claim — surfaced in community discussion on r/AI_Agents and picked up more broadly — that more than 80% of code merged into Anthropic's codebase was authored by Claude. This figure is being cited in conversations about AI replacing knowledge workers.

Ignore this for now. Not because it isn't interesting, but because a single internal metric from one AI company about their own highly specialized engineering workflow tells you very little about what's coming for your job. Software companies writing software with AI tools are the most extreme possible case — they have the infrastructure, the expertise to review AI output, and the incentive to push AI usage to its limits. It does not translate directly to what's happening in marketing, operations, finance, teaching, or most other knowledge work. Keep doing your job well. Use AI where it helps you specifically. Don't make career decisions based on one company's internal engineering stats.
