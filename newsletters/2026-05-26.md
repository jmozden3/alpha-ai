## ⚡ The Alpha — Tip of the Week

**Stop writing AI-generated emails that get ignored immediately.**

Paul Graham — the influential startup investor and essayist — made a pointed observation this week: he can immediately tell when an email is written by AI, and he has never knowingly finished reading one. His words: "It feels like being lied to." The problem isn't using AI to help you communicate. The problem is *replacing* your voice with AI's default voice — that "hard-hitting journalistic style" nobody actually writes in when they're being themselves.

Here's the fix. Use AI to sharpen your thinking, not to replace your drafting. Try this approach before your next important email:

1. Write your email in your own words first — even if it's rough, even if it's just bullet points.
2. Paste it into ChatGPT or Claude with this instruction: *"Clean this up without changing my tone or adding corporate language. Cut anything redundant. Keep it short."*
3. Read the result out loud. If it no longer sounds like you, push back: *"That last paragraph sounds too formal. Make it more direct and personal."*
4. Send the version that still sounds like you wrote it.

The goal is you, edited — not AI, ventriloquizing.

## 🛠 Tool of the Week

**ElevenReader — Turn any document, article, or link into high-quality audio you can listen to on a walk.**

What it does: ElevenReader is an iPhone app that reads any URL or pasted text to you in a natural, high-quality AI voice — highlighting each paragraph as it goes, so you can follow along if you glance at your screen.

How to use it right now:
1. Download ElevenReader from the App Store (free to start).
2. Find any long article, report, or document you've been meaning to read — copy the URL.
3. Open ElevenReader, paste the URL, and press play.
4. Put your phone in your pocket and listen on your commute, during a walk, or while doing something with your hands.

This week, Simon Willison used it to listen to the Pope's full AI encyclical — a dense, lengthy document — on a walk with his dog, and described the experience as working "very well." That's a useful real-world test.

Best for: anyone with a reading backlog and a commute, or anyone who absorbs information better by listening than reading. Especially useful for long industry reports, research summaries, or any document that matters but isn't urgent enough to sit down with.

## 💡 Prompt of the Week

**The "Build My Day" Prompt — Give an AI your calendar and to-do list, and have it sequence your actual day.**

The Rundown AI highlighted a workflow this week around using AI as a personal scheduling assistant. The insight is simple but underused: AI is remarkably good at helping you sequence a messy day if you give it the right raw material. Most people ask AI to write things for them. Far fewer use it to think through *when* to do things — which is often the harder problem.

Here's when to use this: Monday morning, or any time your day feels like an unordered pile of obligations.

Copy and paste this prompt into ChatGPT or Claude, filling in the bracketed sections:

```
I need help planning my day. Here's what I'm working with:

FIXED COMMITMENTS (meetings, calls, deadlines with set times):
[Paste your calendar items with times here]

TASKS I NEED TO GET DONE TODAY (no fixed time, but need to happen):
[List your to-dos here]

ABOUT MY ENERGY: I tend to do my best focused thinking in the [morning/afternoon]. 
I have about [X] hours of actual working time today.

Please sequence my day hour by hour, grouping similar tasks where it makes sense, 
protecting time for my deepest work, and flagging anything that looks unrealistic 
given my available time. Be honest if my list is too long for one day.
```

The "be honest if my list is too long" line is key — it gives the AI permission to tell you what you already suspect but don't want to admit.

## 🔓 Workflow Unlock

**Use Claude or ChatGPT to audit a document — meeting notes, a proposal, a report — in 15 minutes by treating the AI as a critical reader, not a proofreader.**

Most people use AI to improve their writing (fix grammar, make it cleaner). That's fine, but there's a more powerful move: use it to pressure-test your thinking before anyone else does.

Here's the exact workflow:

1. Open Claude at claude.ai (free account works).
2. Paste in whatever you're about to send — a proposal, a summary, a slide deck outline, a set of recommendations.
3. Type this instruction: *"Read this as a skeptical but fair senior colleague. What are the three weakest points in my argument? What questions will my audience immediately ask that I haven't answered? What's the single most important thing I should add or clarify before sharing this?"*
4. Read the response. You don't have to agree with everything — treat it as one smart reader's reaction.
5. Revise the one or two things that genuinely land.

The Rundown AI highlighted a version of this as a 15-minute "context audit" of your work — the core idea being that AI is most useful not when it writes for you, but when it reads for you. A second opinion that's available at midnight, asks nothing in return, and won't be diplomatic to spare your feelings is genuinely useful.

## 📡 Signal vs Noise

**SIGNAL: AI tools that act on your behalf can leak your files to strangers — and this is a real, present risk, not a hypothetical.**

Security researcher reports this week surfaced a vulnerability in Microsoft Copilot Cowork — a product that lets an AI agent send emails and handle files on your behalf. The problem: because the AI could send emails that contained external images, an attacker who could influence what the AI did could cause it to quietly transmit pre-authenticated download links to your files to a server they controlled. In plain English: someone could trick the AI assistant working on your behalf into handing over your documents without you ever knowing.

Why this matters to you right now, even if you're not using this specific product: every major platform is racing to give AI agents the ability to read your files, send emails, and take actions in your name. The more an AI can *do*, the more damage a bad actor can cause if they figure out how to manipulate it. The attack type here is called "prompt injection" — where hidden instructions embedded in a document or email hijack what the AI does next.

What to actually do about this today: be thoughtful about which AI tools you grant access to your email, files, and calendar. When a new AI feature asks for broad permissions, read what it's actually asking for. Convenience now has a real security cost that's worth considering.

*Source: Simon Willison — https://simonwillison.net/2026/May/26/copilot-cowork-exfiltrates-files/#atom-everything*

**NOISE: AI agents completing 35-hour uninterrupted work runs.**

Multiple outlets this week flagged reports of AI systems running autonomously for 35+ hours straight on complex tasks. It sounds dramatic, and the AI enthusiast community is understandably excited. But for the vast majority of knowledge workers, this is a research curiosity, not a tool you'll touch this week or this month. These are highly technical, developer-configured systems — not something you open in a browser tab and point at your inbox. The interesting version of this story is probably 18 months away. Until then, the far more useful question is: what can AI help me finish in the next 20 minutes? That answer is already pretty good.
